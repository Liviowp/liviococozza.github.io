The Price of Merit: When Hard Work Loses Its Value

Degrees, sacrifice and professional recognition in an industry where opportunity can sometimes seem to have a price tag.

For several years now, I have noticed that people with genuine university degrees are losing their standing and recognition in the job market.

A graduate invests time, money and energy to earn a degree—often moving far away from family and friends. You accept difficult compromises: rundown apartments, challenging flatmates and low-paid side jobs just to afford a beer on a Saturday night.

Then you move abroad, adapt to new cultures, learn new languages and build friendships from scratch, all while fighting for that dream career. Huge sacrifices, made for a single ambition: turning a dream into reality.

Yet today, expensive private courses and pay-to-play institutions seem to be taking over. If you come from wealth or have deep financial resources, those sacrifices can become distant memories. Suddenly, everything appears faster, smoother and effortless.

What about those who actually earned their degree?

What about the people already in the job market, trying to compete under these conditions? We find ourselves forced to accumulate credential after credential, knowing full well that our salary may not budge. Or worse, we have to remove courses, conferences and certifications from our CVs just to avoid hearing the usual response: “You are overqualified.”

Every now and then, I look at my CV and think about all the certifications I have had to remove—from anti-doping regulations to sports business—and who knows how many others. The truth is that five pages would not be enough to tell the full story of the work I have put in.

Yet here we are, competing in the sports industry with people who seem to do half the work and still land the job.

Merit should mean more than the ability to pay.

Education is not just a certificate, and professional value cannot be measured by the number of courses someone can afford to attend. Behind a genuine qualification there are years of study, difficult choices, practical experience and personal sacrifices.

Recognising merit means looking beyond appearances and price tags. It means valuing competence, commitment and the journey that brought someone to the point where they are ready to do the job.
